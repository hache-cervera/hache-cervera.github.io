/ Research / TLS certificates / October 2026

Your website's certificate expires. From March, much sooner.

A TLS certificate is a website's ID card: it puts the padlock there and tells the browser it can trust the site. From 15 March 2027 a certificate can last 100 days at most, and from 2029, 47. I measured the certificates of the 1,000 most visited .es sites to see who already renews at that pace and who will have to change habits.

/ 01 / The basics

What a TLS certificate is, assuming nothing

When you open a site over https://, two things happen. What you send travels in a sealed envelope only that site can open. And the site shows a certificate, its ID card, to prove it is who it claims to be. A certificate authority signs it (Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, Google, Spain's FNMT…) after checking that whoever asks for it controls the domain.

Browsers ship with a list of authorities they trust, like someone who knows by heart which official stamps are real. But authorities keep their master stamp locked away and sign with an intermediate certificate. So a site has to show its ID card and also the credentials of the office that issued it. If those credentials are missing, the chain is incomplete.

Why do they expire? In case someone steals the key. Revoking a stolen certificate works poorly in practice, so the best defence is a short life.

/ 02 / What changes

From ID card to yogurt

Browsers and certificate authorities decide this together at the CA/B Forum. The maximum counts from the day a certificate is issued, not from its expiry.

Source: CA/B Forum Baseline Requirements, version 2.3.1, section 6.3.2.

Renewing by hand is like renewing a passport: forget it and you are stuck. For a website, that means the red "your connection is not private" page.

/ 04 / Findings

What caught my eye

  • Your browser covers the gap. 11 sites are missing their intermediate certificate. Chrome downloads it on the spot and Firefox takes it from a list it keeps updated, so both show the padlock. curl, apps, business integrations and bots do neither, and for them the site fails. The owner opens the site, sees the padlock and assumes all is well.
  • A fresh Firefox does fail. My first Firefox run gave 10 errors out of 11 (SEC_ERROR_UNKNOWN_ISSUER). It was a brand new profile, without the intermediate list. With a profile synced for 16 minutes, all 11 loaded. I keep it as a control group: it shows that list is what saves Firefox.
  • The Greek authority behind Spanish universities. 70 .es domains carry certificates from HARICA, the Greek academic network's authority. Since January 2025 HARICA runs the certificate service of GÉANT, the European research network, and RedIRIS offers it in Spain. By domain name they are universities (47), public bodies (11) and research centres (10). I checked 15 at random by hand and 13 matched.
  • The sites already on the 2029 pace didn't choose it. All 19 certificates of 47 days or less last exactly 30 days, and 17 come from Certainly, Fastly's authority, which only works with its CDN and renews every 20 days. The early adopters are early because their CDN does it for them.
  • Two certificates per site. On 226 of 847 domains (26.7%), the www and non-www versions use different certificates. Not a fault, but two things to renew, and the forgotten corner is where expired ones show up.

/ 05 / What can't be measured

Who renews by hand is invisible from outside

I wanted to know which sites renew by hand. I tried it with the public certificate history (Certificate Transparency, queried through crt.sh) on a random subsample of 100 domains, with thresholds fixed before looking at results.

It didn't work. Large sites hold dozens of certificates at once (their CDN, several providers, one per product), crt.sh misses wildcard and multi-domain certificates, and even Let's Encrypt, which renews on its own by design, came out as "manual". If a method gets wrong what you know for sure, you can't trust the rest. I dropped it without publishing a figure and without moving the thresholds.

That's why 48.4% is the share of sites whose issuer renews on its own, not measured renewals. The rest may be automated too: from outside you can't tell.

/ 06 / How to fix it

Have the new ID card arrive by post

StepHow
Look at your certificateIn the browser, the icon left of the address, "Connection is secure", "Certificate is valid". It shows who issued it and when it expires.
Check it the way an app doesopenssl s_client -connect yourdomain.com:443 -servername yourdomain.com should end in Verify return code: 0 (ok). If it says unable to verify the first certificate, the intermediate is missing. Without installing anything: SSL Labs flags it as "Chain issues: Incomplete".
Automate renewalWith ACME, the protocol Let's Encrypt uses: certbot or acme.sh on your server, or your host or CDN if they already do it. It's free.
Send the full chainYour server has to send your certificate and the intermediate. With certbot, use fullchain.pem instead of cert.pem.
Watch the expiry dateEven when automated: automation fails too, and you'd rather find out before your customers do.
Check both versionsWith www and without it, plus any subdomain still in DNS.

I'm building yogurTLS, a free one-command checker that tells you how your certificate is doing, what changes in 2027 and how to fix whatever is wrong.

/ 07 / Method

How I measured it, and its limits

  • Sample. The first 1,000 domains ending in .es on Tranco list K9Z7W. It starts at rank 724, because many large Spanish sites use .com: this is the most visited .es web, not the whole Spanish web.
  • Unit. The domain, measured at the home page's final destination after redirects (949 of 1,000; the other 51 at the default host). Both www and non-www are kept raw.
  • Connection. One normal TLS handshake per host with Node 20, like any browser. No path or port scanning.
  • Errors. Every invalid certificate was measured again and opened in a real Chrome 154 to confirm the cause. The 11 incomplete chains were also tested in Firefox 157, with a fresh profile and a synced one.
  • No secure connection. 42 domains: 8 sites (living at another address or only unencrypted), 9 technical domains, classified by name, and 25 with no visible site.
  • Limits. One network location, one day: a CDN may serve a different certificate depending on where you connect from. Two sites fixed their certificate the next day; they count as they were on the 9th.

Sources: CA/B Forum, Baseline Requirements, Tranco list K9Z7W, GÉANT Trusted Certificate Service, RedIRIS, TCS authorities, Fastly, Certainly and RFC 8555, ACME.
