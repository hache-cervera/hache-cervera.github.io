/ TLS certificates

Check when your website's TLS certificate expires

Your website's TLS certificate expires, and sooner every year. From 15 March 2027 it can last 100 days at most, and from 2029, 47. If you renew by hand, that's going from once a year to at least four times, then around eight.

/ How to fix it

How to renew your TLS certificate without having to remember

Renewing by hand is like renewing a passport: forget it and you are stuck. What you want is the new one arriving by post. This gets you there, and all of it is free.

StepHow
Automate renewalWith ACME, the protocol Let's Encrypt uses: certbot or acme.sh on your server, or your host or CDN if they already do it. At 47 days, renewing by hand stops being an option.
Send the full chainYour server has to send your certificate and the intermediate. With certbot, use fullchain.pem instead of cert.pem.
Check it outside the browserWith yogurTLS, or with openssl s_client -connect yourdomain.com:443 -servername yourdomain.com, which should end in Verify return code: 0 (ok). Without installing anything, SSL Labs flags an incomplete chain as "Chain issues: Incomplete".
Watch the expiry dateEven when automated, because automation fails too. npx yogurtls yourdomain.com --warn-days 14 in a cron job warns you in time.
Check every versionWith www, without it, and any subdomain still in DNS.

/ 01 / The basics

What a TLS certificate is, assuming nothing

When you open a site over https://, two things happen. What you send travels in a sealed envelope only that site can open. And the site shows a certificate, its ID card, to prove it is who it claims to be. A certificate authority signs it (Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, Google, Spain's FNMT…) after checking that whoever asks for it controls the domain.

Browsers ship with a list of authorities they trust, like someone who knows by heart which official stamps are real. But authorities keep their master stamp locked away and sign with an intermediate certificate. So a site has to show its ID card and also the credentials of the office that issued it. If those credentials are missing, the chain is incomplete.

Why do they expire? In case someone steals the key. Revoking a stolen certificate works poorly in practice, so the best defence is a short life.

/ 02 / What changes

What changes in 2027 and 2029

Browsers and certificate authorities decide this together at the CA/B Forum. The maximum counts from the day a certificate is issued, not from its expiry. It's going from an ID card to a yogurt.

Source: CA/B Forum Baseline Requirements, version 2.3.1, section 6.3.2.

/ 04 / Findings

What's worth knowing about your TLS certificate

  • Your browser covers the gap. 11 sites are missing their intermediate certificate. Chrome downloads it on the spot and Firefox takes it from a list it keeps updated, so both show the padlock. curl, apps, business integrations and bots do neither, and for them the site fails. You open your site, see the padlock and assume all is well.
  • A fresh Firefox does fail. With a brand new profile, without that list, 10 of the 11 failed (SEC_ERROR_UNKNOWN_ISSUER). With a profile synced for 16 minutes, all 11 loaded. That's the control group: the list is what saves Firefox.
  • The Greek authority behind Spanish universities. 70 .es domains carry certificates from HARICA, the Greek academic network's authority. Since January 2025 HARICA runs the certificate service of GÉANT, the European research network, and RedIRIS offers it in Spain. By domain name they are universities (47), public bodies (11) and research centres (10); I checked 15 at random by hand and 13 matched. If you work at a university, this rule hits you directly.
  • The sites already on the 2029 pace didn't choose it. All 19 certificates of 47 days or less last exactly 30 days, and 17 come from Certainly, Fastly's authority, which only works with its CDN and renews every 20 days. If your CDN or host renews for you, the 2027 change won't touch you.
  • Two certificates per site. On 226 of 847 domains (26.7%), the www and non-www versions use different certificates. Not a fault, but two things to renew.

/ 05 / What can't be measured

Who renews by hand is invisible from outside

I tried to tell which sites renew by hand using the public certificate history (Certificate Transparency, queried through crt.sh), on a random subsample of 100 domains, with thresholds fixed before looking at results.

It didn't work. Large sites hold dozens of certificates at once, crt.sh misses wildcard and multi-domain certificates, and even Let's Encrypt, which renews on its own by design, came out as "manual". If a method gets wrong what you know for sure, you can't trust the rest, so I dropped it without publishing a figure.

That's why 48.4% is the share of sites whose issuer renews on its own, not measured renewals. It's also why yogurTLS doesn't tell you whether your renewal is manual or automatic: only what can be measured.

  • Sample. The first 1,000 domains ending in .es on Tranco list K9Z7W. It starts at rank 724, because many large Spanish sites use .com: this is the most visited .es web, not the whole Spanish web.
  • Unit. The domain, measured at the home page's final destination after redirects (949 of 1,000; the other 51 at the default host).
  • Connection. One normal TLS handshake per host, like any browser. No path or port scanning.
  • Errors. Every invalid certificate was measured again and opened in a real Chrome to confirm the cause. The 11 incomplete chains were also tested in Firefox, with a fresh profile and a synced one.
  • No secure connection. 42 domains: 8 sites (living at another address or only unencrypted), 9 technical domains, classified by name, and 25 with no visible site.
  • Limits. One network location, one day: a CDN may serve a different certificate depending on where you connect from. Two sites fixed their certificate the next day; they count as they were on the 9th.
  • No names. Neither this page nor the downloadable data names any site with a broken certificate.
