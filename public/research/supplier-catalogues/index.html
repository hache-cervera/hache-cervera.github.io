What do supplier catalogues actually contain?

An engine spare parts company in Spain wanted its products in an online shop. Nobody sends it product data through an API: it gets PDF catalogues, a listing from another shop and a price list. I counted what arrives in those files, field by field, across 37,948 products.

/ 01 · What arrives

A name and a price, and little else

Every product has a name, and 91% end up with a price. That is the easy part. Only 1,434 products, 3.8%, come with a description and a list of compatible engines, and all of them come from the PDF catalogues. Not a single file brings a photo, a weight, a barcode, stock, a lead time, a cost price or cross-references to other brands.

Each empty column becomes a decision in the shop. Without weight, shipping cannot be calculated by weight. Without stock or lead time, the honest button is “ask about availability”, not “add to cart”. Without photos, the shop has to choose between a generic image and no image.

Names also arrive in Spanish, German and English. After the tool translates them, 62 still look foreign and are flagged for a person to check.

/ 02 · Five sources

The biggest source is the emptiest

Source C, a listing of original parts saved from an online shop, has 107,632 rows. 71,118 are dropped because they are not engine parts, and the remaining 36,514 are 96% of the catalogue. They come with a name, a reference and a price, and nothing else: no description, no compatible engines, not even a family. The tool works out the family from the name for 35,970 of them.

The PDF catalogues are the opposite: descriptions and engines, but no prices. Catalogues B and D get their price from list E by joining on the reference: 1,259 of 1,289 and 135 of 137.

/ 03 · In the shop

Where the parts go, and what picture they get

The 37,948 products become 34,339 shop listings (34,379 variants; only 32 listings have more than one). Almost all of them, 34,333, say which vehicle make they fit. Only 559, 1.6%, name a model, and 450 an engine code.

With no photos, each listing gets one generic image for its family, when there is one: 22,211 listings, 65%. Coverage depends on the system. A gasket looks like a gasket; a sensor or a workshop tool does not look like the next one.

/ 04 · Two tests

Two fixes that looked good on paper

Image by name

If a listing has no family image, the tool could look for one by words in the name. On the full catalogue that takes listings with an image from 22,211 to 25,311, 3,100 more. I reviewed a sample of 15 by hand: 14 showed the wrong part. A wrong photo on a spare part is worse than none, so it stays an advanced option, off by default.

Filtering by whole words

The filter that drops what is not an engine part matches pieces of words. Matching whole words looks cleaner, but on the full files it removes 840 products, 420 of them springs, and lets in 75 new ones, such as cable grommets. 765 fewer products in total. Which products the shop should sell is the client’s call, not a code change, so the filter stays as it is.

/ 05 · Method

How it was counted, and its limits

  • One company, five sources. It describes this case, not every supplier in the sector.
  • Counts come from the catalogue tool’s own output on the full files, run on 25 October 2026. A field counts as present when it is not empty.
  • The sources are anonymised as A to E. Product-level data is the client’s and is not published; the downloadable files only hold the counts on this page.
  • Prices are market prices from source E and the online shop listing, not the distributor’s own tariff.
  • The image test was checked on a sample of 15 listings, enough to see the problem, not to measure its exact size.
