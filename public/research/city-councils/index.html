How slow and how accessible are city council websites?

I measured the home pages of the city councils of Spain’s 50 provincial capitals: how long they take on an average phone, how much they weigh, and how they work for someone who cannot see the screen. These are public websites; people need them to book appointments and get certificates.

/ 01 · Speed

A national experience, measured

The median mobile score is 56 out of 100 and 11 capitals score under 50. The median home page needs 17 seconds to show its main content; Google calls anything over 4 seconds slow. The median page weighs almost 6 MB and 12 weigh more than 10 MB. Bilbao and Vitoria-Gasteiz were the fastest in this run.

The heaviest home page is Valladolid’s, 34 MB, and it still scores 57: 32 of those megabytes are two videos that download on their own, but the page appears in 13 seconds. Murcia weighs 30 MB, scores 4 and, in the simulation, takes over two minutes. The score measures when you can see and use the page, not how much it weighs.

In Salamanca a single photo of an electric car weighs 21.8 MB, more than 46 of the 50 home pages. In Murcia a press-release photo weighs 5.9 MB. Images uploaded without anyone checking their size.

/ 02 · Accessibility

Using them without seeing the screen

axe found WCAG 2.1 errors on 42 of 49 home pages on desktop (23 with critical errors) and on 46 of 49 on mobile (28 critical). Toledo was down during the desktop run and Huesca during the mobile one. Automated tests only find part of the problems: Deque measured about 57% of issues by volume, so the real picture is worse, not better.

The clearest case: on mobile, the menu button in Donostia, Huelva and Toledo has no name, so a screen reader just says “button”. In Huelva the button does include the text “Toggle navigation” for screen readers, but a mobile CSS rule, .navbar-toggle span { display: none !important }, hides it, and the accessible name ends up empty. In Palma axe flags 146 elements, most of them links without text: service icons whose label sits outside the link.

Accessibility declarations: 24 say “partially compliant”, none claims full compliance, 6 still cite standards that have been replaced, 13 could not be found from the home page and 7 do not state their level.

/ 04 · WCAG

What WCAG is, version by version

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the W3C’s technical standard for making websites usable by people with disabilities: blind people using screen readers, people who navigate only with a keyboard, people with low vision, deaf people, people with cognitive disabilities. They are organised around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust.

Each rule is a “success criterion” with a level: A (the minimum), AA and AAA. Laws ask for level AA, which means meeting every A and AA criterion. No law requires AAA as a whole.

VersionYearWhat it added
WCAG 1.01999The first version, written for the HTML of the time. Replaced.
WCAG 2.02008Technology-neutral rules and the A/AA/AAA levels. Also ISO/IEC 40500:2012.
WCAG 2.1201817 new criteria for mobile, low vision and cognitive needs: reflow at 320 px (1.4.10), contrast of icons and controls (1.4.11), text spacing, screen orientation, pointer gestures, status messages.
WCAG 2.220239 new criteria: focus not hidden behind sticky bars (2.4.11), alternatives to dragging (2.5.7), targets of at least 24 px (2.5.8), help in a consistent place, not asking twice for the same data, and logins without puzzles (3.3.8). Removes 4.1.1 Parsing.
WCAG 3.0draftA full rewrite still in progress at the W3C. Not a standard yet.

/ 05 · The law

Which version is mandatory, and where

WhereWhoWhat it requires
Spain, public sectorPublic administration websites and apps (RD 1112/2018, from the EU Web Accessibility Directive 2016/2102)WCAG 2.1 AA through EN 301 549 v3.2.1, an accessibility declaration linked as “Accessibility” from every page, a complaints channel and periodic reviews
Spain, private sectorCertain products and services: e-commerce, banking, transport, e-books, telecoms (Law 11/2023, from the European Accessibility Act 2019/882), applicable since 28 June 2025The same EN 301 549 standard. Micro-enterprises providing services are exempt
European Union, nextBoth of the aboveEN 301 549 v4.1.1, published on 2 September 2026, moves to WCAG 2.2. It becomes binding once cited in the Official Journal of the EU, expected on 16 December 2026
United KingdomPublic sector bodiesWCAG 2.2 AA
United StatesState and local governments (ADA Title II, 2024 rule)WCAG 2.1 AA. An April 2026 interim rule moved the deadlines to April 2027 (50,000+ inhabitants) and April 2028 (smaller entities)

So the 50 city councils in this study have had to meet WCAG 2.1 AA since 2018, and will have to meet 2.2 once the new standard is cited. WCAG is the floor, not the ceiling, and many of these sites do not reach the floor.

/ 06 · Method

How it was measured, and its limits

  • Speed: Lighthouse, the engine behind PageSpeed Insights, on a simulated mid-range phone with slow 4G. Each site three times, median kept, about 150 runs. Re-checked by hand in PageSpeed: most moved less than 10 points; Vitoria moved most, from 92 to 65. Trust the order more than the exact number.
  • Weight is the most stable figure: 30 MB is 30 MB whoever measures it.
  • It is a lab test with a simulated phone, not real-user data, and only the home page.
  • almeria.es belongs to the provincial council, not the city: the right site had to be found by hand.
  • Accessibility: axe-core 4.13 against WCAG 2.1 A and AA. Two detectors were discarded for not being reliable enough: invisible focus and “skip to content”.
