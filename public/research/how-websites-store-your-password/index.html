How long would it take to steal your password?

When a website's database gets stolen, what decides whether your password breaks in seconds or never breaks at all? I stored the same 20 passwords the four ways websites do and attacked them from a laptop. The result depends on the password and on how the website stores it, and the person signing up does not choose the second.

/ 01 · Signing up

What happens to your password when you sign up

A well-built website does not keep your password. It keeps a hash: the result of running it through a formula that cannot be reversed. It takes five steps.

  1. 1

    You type the password

    Into the form. It travels to the server encrypted over HTTPS.

  2. 2

    The server generates a salt

    A random value, different for each user.

  3. 3

    It runs it through the formula

    Password and salt, at whatever cost the developer chose. This step sets what each guess costs.

  4. 4

    It stores a single line

    Algorithm, cost, salt and hash together. bcrypt at cost 12 starts with $2y$12$; Argon2id spells out its settings: $argon2id$v=19$m=47104,t=1,p=1$.

  5. 5

    It forgets the password

    When you come back, it repeats the sum with the stored salt and compares. If it matches, you are in.

Whoever steals the database takes those lines. They do not see your password: they have to guess it, hash each guess and compare. What each guess costs them was decided by the website in step 3.

A different scenario

Normal login

  • The attacker guesses through the site's form, one at a time.
  • The site can stop them: attempt limits, temporary lockouts, a second factor.
  • What decides it: those defences, and your password not being one of the most common.

What this test measures

Stolen database

  • The attacker has the hashes on their own machine and needs no one's permission.
  • There is no attempt limit, and a second factor does not help.
  • What decides it: how the password was stored, and how predictable it is.

/ 02 · The four methods

What each storage method is

All four methods in the test produce a hash, but they were built for different jobs.

MethodWhat it isWhat it was built forFit for passwords?
MD5A 128-bit digest from 1992. Very fast to compute.Checking that a file has not changed, like a fingerprint. It is broken even for that today.No. It is fast on purpose.
SHA-256A 256-bit digest from the SHA-2 family (2001). Fast, and sound as a fingerprint.Digital signatures, TLS certificates, file integrity.Not on its own. It is fast on purpose too.
bcryptA 1999 formula made for passwords. It is salted and has a cost: each step of cost doubles the work. Cost 12 means 4,096 repetitions.Storing passwords.Yes. OWASP asks for cost 10 at least.
Argon2idThe winner of the 2015 Password Hashing Competition. Salted, and it demands memory on top of time for every guess.Storing passwords, including against graphics cards.Yes. It is OWASP's first choice.

A salt means two people with the same password end up with different hashes, so the attacker cannot crack them both at once. In the test, MD5 and SHA-256 were unsalted, as on many older sites.

Why the speeds come out like this

  • MD5 and SHA-256 are short sums. A graphics card has thousands of small cores doing the same operation at once, and these formulas barely touch memory. That is how the RTX 4060 manages billions a second. SHA-256 runs about 3.9 times slower because its sum is longer.
  • bcrypt is expensive on purpose. At cost 12 it repeats its setup 4,096 times and, all the way through, keeps reading and writing a 4 KB table. Graphics cards handle that kind of memory access badly: many of their cores sit waiting.
  • Argon2id demands memory. Every guess needs 46 MiB. The laptop RTX 4060 has 8 GB, so at most about 170 guesses fit at once (a calculation). But with a single pass, each guess walks that memory once, and moving memory is exactly what a graphics card does fast.

Speeds measured during the attack, not taken from the tool benchmark. For MD5 and SHA-256 the top speed comes from the brute-force phase: in the dictionary phase the limit was reading the list from disk, about 500 million guesses a second. For bcrypt and Argon2id the hash itself is the limit in every phase. A machine with more or better cards would be faster; this test does not measure by how much.

/ 04 · The attack

What it was attacked with: a leaked list, combinations and brute force

An attacker does not guess at random. They start with passwords people have already used and leave character-by-character guessing for last. The test did the same, in this order and with the same pieces against all four methods:

  1. Phase 1: dictionary

    rockyou

    14,344,391 user passwords from a 2009 breach, public ever since. The base of almost any attack.

  2. Phase 1: dictionary

    best66 rules

    The usual tweaks to every rockyou word: capital first letter, letters for digits, suffixes.

  3. Phase 2: combinations

    354 custom pieces

    One Piece names, common English words, the years 1990 to 2026 and suffixes such as !, 123, @ or 404.

  4. Phase 2: combinations

    princeprocessor

    Joins those pieces in twos and threes. It is what built CrazyCatLady.

  5. Phase 3: brute force

    Mask

    Character by character, about 20 minutes. Only against MD5 and SHA-256: bcrypt, at 317 a second, would not cover even 4 characters (a calculation).

None of the 20 passwords was in the list of 354 pieces, checked in code. The common ones could be in rockyou naturally, and that was exactly what I wanted to measure. CrazyCatLady was not on any list: the program built it by joining crazy, cat and lady.

What fell, and why

MD5 and SHA-256 lost the same three. bcrypt and Argon2id lost none, including the two that were in rockyou.

Each time is the end of the phase in which the password fell, counted from the start of the attack: it fell within that time. With bcrypt and Argon2id the dictionary did not even finish within the 10 minutes per phase. At 317 guesses a second, going through rockyou and its variations would take up to about 35 days; at 2,478, about 4.

Three things I did not expect

  • ZoroGotLostAg@in!404 held, even with MD5. It has capitals, symbols and numbers. CrazyCatLady, with no symbols, fell because it is three common words.
  • PleaseUseMe looked common and held. It was not in rockyou, and the dictionary variations never reached it.
  • PorqueFi held, and it is still weak. It has 8 characters, letters only. The test brute force reached 6 or 7 characters; trying letters only, MD5 would find it in about 1.3 hours (a calculation).

/ 07 · Configuration

The algorithm's configuration decides

Saying "this site uses bcrypt" or "it uses MD5" is not enough. What sets the price of each guess is the parameters it runs with: the cost, the memory, the passes, the salt.

Argon2id, the newer one, held less than bcrypt

With the test settings, bcrypt held about 8 times longer: 317 guesses a second, against 2,478 for Argon2id. Argon2id ran at the minimum OWASP recommends (46 MiB and one pass, the first option on its list). bcrypt ran above its own: cost 12, where the minimum is 10. Argon2id with more memory or more passes would be slower to attack, and bcrypt at cost 10 about 4 times faster (a calculation: each step of cost doubles the work). Raising the parameters has a price: the server takes longer on every login.

Not every "MD5" is the MD5 in this test

Up to version 6.7, WordPress used a scheme called phpass. Inside it is MD5, but salted and repeated 8,192 times. The name is the same, but it allows roughly 8,000 times fewer guesses a second. It is still thousands of times faster to attack than bcrypt at cost 12.

11,350 Mguesses per second: the test's unsalted MD5 (measured)
≈ 1.4 Mguesses per second: WordPress phpass up to 6.7 (a calculation, not measured)
317guesses per second: bcrypt at cost 12 (measured)

One bcrypt limit: it only uses the first 72 bytes of a password. The 68-character one in the test just fits. That is why WordPress 6.8 first condenses the password with SHA-384 and hands that to bcrypt.

/ 08 · In CMSs

How WordPress, Drupal and the rest store passwords

Most websites do not write this code: they inherit it from their content management system. This is how it has changed in the most used ones.

On my own site, devilfruittcg.com, running WordPress: I checked, and it stores passwords with bcrypt.

The hash only comes into play once the database has been stolen. Other defences come first: keeping the CMS and plugins updated, since that is how databases usually get stolen; limiting login attempts, which WordPress does not do out of the box (a plugin or the proxy adds it); and a second factor on admin accounts.

/ 09 · The LastPass case

The same problem, in a password manager

In 2022, attackers stole backups of LastPass users' vaults. It was not a website with a table of hashes, but the failure is the one this test measures: how much work each guess costs.

  • Each vault key comes from the master password through PBKDF2, which repeats a fast hash from the SHA family. The repetitions, which LastPass calls iterations, act as the cost.
  • The default went up, but older accounts were not updated automatically. Some sat at 5,000, at 500 and even at 1.
  • The saved website addresses were not encrypted. Passwords and notes were. The attacker could see which sites each entry belonged to and choose which vaults to attack first.
  • LastPass spoke of "millions of years" to crack a master password. Analyst Wladimir Palant replied that this confuses pure brute force with a password a person chooses: on a single graphics card, a master password with 40 bits of entropy would fall in about two months, by his estimate.

How it differs from this test: here bcrypt is compared with MD5 on a website; at LastPass it was PBKDF2 with few iterations in a password manager. The underlying problem is the same, a fast formula with little work per guess. And the same thing happens as with WordPress: raising the cost only protects the accounts that get migrated.

/ 10 · What to do

How to protect yourself better

If you have accounts on websites

  • A different password for every site. If a site stores it with MD5 and gets breached, that password falls in seconds. If you use it elsewhere, every account falls with it.
  • A password manager to keep them. That way each site gets its own random password and you do not have to memorise any. Make the master password long and used nowhere else: at LastPass, each vault key came from it.
  • For the ones you memorise, a long phrase of your own, rather than something like P@ssw0rd!. Not built from pieces everyone uses.
  • A second factor wherever you can. It does not save a stolen password, but it stops anyone logging in with the password alone.

If you build websites

  • bcrypt or Argon2id, and check the parameters: they decide what each guess costs. The OWASP guide gives minimum values for each algorithm. MD5 and SHA-256 on their own are not fit for passwords.
  • Rehash old hashes at login, as WordPress 6.8 does, and decide what happens to accounts that never come back.
  • Limit login attempts and offer a second factor. That covers the other scenario, the sign-in screen.
  • Look at the password column of your database at least once. It takes a minute.

/ 11 · Method

How it was measured, and its limits

  • 20 passwords are not a sample. They were picked by hand to test ideas; the results describe these 20, not passwords in general.
  • The same 20, stored all four ways with Python (hashlib, bcrypt and argon2-cffi): MD5 and SHA-256 unsalted, bcrypt at cost 12 and Argon2id with 46 MiB, 1 pass and 1 thread.
  • A laptop with an RTX 4060 and hashcat 7.0.0. Speeds are the ones measured during the attack.
  • Three phases per storage method: rockyou with the best66 rules; combinations from the 354-piece list with princeprocessor (none of the 20 was in it, checked in code); and mask brute force for MD5 and SHA-256 only.
  • Limited time: about 20 minutes of brute force on MD5 and SHA-256 (21 and 25 minutes in practice), 10 minutes per phase on bcrypt and Argon2id. A password that did not fall means it did not fall in that time.
  • The 35 and 4 days, the 1.4 million for phpass and the brute-force times are calculations, not measurements.
  • The CMS and LastPass details come from the sources below and from Joomla's and PrestaShop's published code, not from this test.
